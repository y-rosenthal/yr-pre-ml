10. Filtering rows#

A manager rarely wants every row. “Show me the big orders.” “How many Electronics sales had a discount?” “List the sales of the North stores.” Each question keeps only the rows that match a condition. This is called filtering, and pandas does it with the Boolean masks that we met for arrays. At the end, we use the same idea to change the values of the matching rows, and we learn the one safe way to do it.

In this chapter, you will learn to:

  • make a mask from a comparison on a column, and keep the rows where it is True;

  • count the rows that match a condition;

  • combine conditions with &, | and ~, and test for several values with isin;

  • filter rows and choose columns in one step with loc;

  • change the values of the matching rows with df.loc[mask, "col"] = value, and avoid the chained-assignment trap.

import pandas as pd

DATA_URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/y-rosenthal/yr-pre-ml/main/data/"

We use the sales of a store chain: 2,000 sales in one year, one row for each sale (see the chapter Reading and describing data).

sales = pd.read_csv(DATA_URL + "sales.csv")
sales.head()
date store region category units price discount
0 2025-01-01 S1 North Clothing 3 46.99 0.1
1 2025-01-01 S8 West Grocery 9 2.99 0.0
2 2025-01-01 S2 North Grocery 11 5.99 0.0
3 2025-01-01 S8 West Grocery 9 11.99 0.0
4 2025-01-01 S5 East Grocery 13 3.99 0.2

10.1. A comparison gives a mask#

Which sales had more than 10 units? We compare the whole column with 10:

many_units = sales["units"] > 10
many_units.head()

0    False
1    False
2     True
3    False
4     True
Name: units, dtype: bool

The result is a Series of True and False, one for each row, with the same index as sales. Compare it with the table above: the rows 2 and 4 have 11 and 13 units, so they are True; the others have 10 units or fewer. Its dtype is bool.

This is a mask, exactly as for NumPy arrays in the chapter Comparisons and Boolean masks. The only difference is that a pandas mask also carries the row labels.

10.2. Keeping the rows that match#

Put the mask inside square brackets, and pandas keeps the rows where the mask is True:

big_sales = sales[sales["units"] > 10]
big_sales.head()
date store region category units price discount
2 2025-01-01 S2 North Grocery 11 5.99 0.0
4 2025-01-01 S5 East Grocery 13 3.99 0.2
11 2025-01-03 S5 East Grocery 11 13.99 0.0
47 2025-01-12 S1 North Grocery 11 10.99 0.0
79 2025-01-19 S4 South Grocery 11 12.99 0.1

Read sales[sales["units"] > 10] as “the rows of sales where units is above 10”. The inner part is the mask from above. Every row of big_sales has more than 10 units.

Look at the index: 2, 4, 11, 47, … The rows keep their labels from sales, as they did after sorting in the chapter Selecting with loc and iloc. So big_sales.iloc[0] is the row with the label 2.

10.3. Counting the rows that match#

How many sales had more than 10 units? There are two ways:

print(many_units.sum())
print(len(big_sales))

75
75

Both say 75. many_units.sum() adds up the mask: True counts as 1 and False as 0, so the sum is the number of True values. len(big_sales) counts the rows of the filtered table. Use the first one when you need only the number.

Try it: many_units.mean() gives the share of the sales with more than 10 units. What share is it, as a percentage?

10.4. Conditions on text#

A condition can compare a text column too. Here are the sales of store S1:

s1_sales = sales[sales["store"] == "S1"]
len(s1_sales)

287

Store S1 made 287 of the 2,000 sales. Note the two equal signs: == compares, and = assigns.

Common mistake: writing sales[sales["store"] = "S1"] with one =. Python stops with a SyntaxError before it runs the cell. And text must match exactly: sales["store"] == "s1" (a small “s”) matches no row at all, without any error.

10.5. Two conditions at once: &#

Which Electronics sales had a discount? We need two conditions, and both must be true. As for arrays, we put each condition in parentheses and join them with & (“and”):

electronics_discount = (sales["category"] == "Electronics") & (sales["discount"] > 0)
electronics_discount.sum()

np.int64(154)

154 sales were Electronics sales with a discount. (Colab shows the sum as np.int64(154), a NumPy whole number, as for arrays.)

The parentheses are needed. Python computes & before == and >, so without them it would try to compute "Electronics" & sales["discount"] first, and stop with an error.

Common mistake: writing and instead of &. Python’s and works on one True or False, not on a whole column, so pandas stops with ValueError: The truth value of a Series is ambiguous. With masks, always use &, | and ~.

10.6. Or, and not: | and ~#

| means “or”: the row is kept when at least one condition is true. ~ means “not”: it turns every True into False and every False into True.

s1_or_s2 = (sales["store"] == "S1") | (sales["store"] == "S2")
not_grocery = ~(sales["category"] == "Grocery")
print(s1_or_s2.sum())
print(not_grocery.sum())

556
1206

556 sales were made in store S1 or store S2 (287 + 269). 1,206 sales were not Grocery sales: Clothing, Home and Electronics together.

10.7. Several values: isin#

To keep the rows whose value is one of several, a chain of | gets long. isin takes a list of values:

three_stores = sales["store"].isin(["S1", "S2", "S5"])
three_stores.sum()

np.int64(817)

isin gives a mask that is True where the store is S1, S2 or S5: 817 sales. It is the same as (sales["store"] == "S1") | (sales["store"] == "S2") | (sales["store"] == "S5"), but shorter and easier to read.

10.8. Rows and columns at once with loc#

Often we want the matching rows, but only some of the columns. In the chapter Selecting with loc and iloc, we wrote df.loc[rows, columns] with labels. The row place also accepts a mask:

sales.loc[sales["units"] > 10, ["date", "store", "units"]].head()
date store units
2 2025-01-01 S2 11
4 2025-01-01 S5 13
11 2025-01-03 S5 11
47 2025-01-12 S1 11
79 2025-01-19 S4 11

The rows with more than 10 units, and only the three columns that we named. With one column name instead of a list, we get a Series, and we can summarize it: sales.loc[sales["units"] > 10, "price"].mean() is the average price in the big sales.

Try it: compare the average price of the sales with more than 10 units and of the sales with 10 units or fewer. Are big sales usually cheap products?

10.9. Changing the matching rows#

loc can also change the matching rows. A manager asks: “What if store S1 had given every customer a 50% discount?” We try it on a copy, sales2, so that sales keeps the real data:

sales2 = sales.copy()
sales2.loc[sales2["store"] == "S1", "discount"] = 0.5
sales2.head()
date store region category units price discount
0 2025-01-01 S1 North Clothing 3 46.99 0.5
1 2025-01-01 S8 West Grocery 9 2.99 0.0
2 2025-01-01 S2 North Grocery 11 5.99 0.0
3 2025-01-01 S8 West Grocery 9 11.99 0.0
4 2025-01-01 S5 East Grocery 13 3.99 0.2

Row 0 is a sale of store S1, and its discount is now 0.5. The other rows of the first five are from other stores, and they did not change.

Why copy()? sales2 = sales would not make a new table: it would give the same table a second name, as with lists in Python, and a change through sales2 would change sales too. copy() makes a separate table, as it did for arrays in the chapter Indexing, slicing and arithmetic.

Let’s check both tables:

print((sales2["discount"] == 0.5).sum())
print((sales["discount"] == 0.5).sum())

287
0

All 287 sales of store S1 now have a discount of 0.5 in sales2, and sales has none. This is the rule for changing values:

Note: to change the rows that match a condition, write df.loc[mask, "col"] = value: the mask, the column and the new value, in one step.

10.10. The chained-assignment trap#

Many people first try something like this (it is wrong, so we do not run it):

sales2[sales2["store"] == "S1"]["discount"] = 0.5     # wrong: does not change sales2

It looks reasonable, but it has two steps. First, sales2[sales2["store"] == "S1"] makes a new table, with only the rows of store S1. Then ["discount"] = 0.5 changes the column of that new table. The new table has no name, so it is thrown away at once, and sales2 stays as it was. This is called chained assignment: two selections in a chain, then =.

In pandas 3, chained assignment never changes sales2, and pandas shows a warning. In pandas 2 it does not change sales2 here either, but the same idea in the other order, sales2["discount"][mask] = 0.5, does change it (with a warning). So code that seems to work in pandas 2 stops working in pandas 3, and only a warning tells you. The warnings also differ between the versions, which is why we show this code without running it.

The fix is always the same: use loc, with the mask and the column in one pair of brackets, sales2.loc[sales2["store"] == "S1", "discount"] = 0.5.

10.11. Summary#

  • A comparison on a column, such as sales["units"] > 10, gives a mask: a Boolean Series with one True/False for each row.

  • sales[mask] keeps the rows where the mask is True; they keep their labels.

  • mask.sum() counts the matching rows (and len(sales[mask]) too); mask.mean() gives their share.

  • Combine conditions with & (and), | (or), ~ (not), with parentheses around each condition: (sales["category"] == "Electronics") & (sales["discount"] > 0).

  • sales["store"].isin(["S1", "S2"]) is True where the value is one of the list.

  • sales.loc[mask, ["date", "store", "units"]] filters rows and chooses columns at once.

  • df.loc[mask, "col"] = value changes the matching rows. Work on a copy (sales2 = sales.copy()) to keep the original. Never chain two selections before =.

no_discount = sales[~(sales["discount"] > 0)]

10.12.5. Question 5 (Medium)#

Show the Clothing and Home sales of store S3. Use isin for the two categories. How many are there?

Answer

isin handles the two categories, and & adds the store. sales["category"].isin(...) needs no parentheses of its own, because it is a method call, not a comparison. Store S3 made 120 Clothing and Home sales.

s3_clothing_home = sales[(sales["store"] == "S3") & sales["category"].isin(["Clothing", "Home"])]
print(s3_clothing_home.head())
print(len(s3_clothing_home))

10.12.8. Question 8 (Hard)#

In a copy of sales, give every Electronics sale in December a discount of 0.2. How many rows did you change? (Hint: the dates are text such as "2025-12-05", and text in this form can be compared: "2025-12-05" >= "2025-12-01" is True.)

Answer

Make the copy first. The December sales are the ones whose date is "2025-12-01" or later. Then change the matching rows with loc, in one step. The mask matches 19 sales, so loc set the discount of 19 rows.

sales3 = sales.copy()
december_electronics = (sales3["category"] == "Electronics") & (sales3["date"] >= "2025-12-01")
sales3.loc[december_electronics, "discount"] = 0.2
print(december_electronics.sum())
print(sales3.loc[december_electronics, ["date", "category", "discount"]].head())

10.12.9. Question 9 (Challenge)#

For each store, count the sales with more than 10 units. Which store had the most? Use a loop over the store names, which sorted(sales["store"].unique()) gives in order.

Answer

For each store, make a mask with two conditions, and add it up. Store S1 had the most sales with more than 10 units: 16.

for store in sorted(sales["store"].unique()):
    count = ((sales["store"] == store) & (sales["units"] > 10)).sum()
    print(store, count)
