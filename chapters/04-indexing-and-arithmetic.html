4. Indexing, slicing and arithmetic#

In the previous chapter, we made arrays and did arithmetic on all their values at once. In this chapter, we pick out one value or a part of an array, change values, and compute with two arrays together: for example, the revenue of each product from its price and its units sold. We also meet a few NumPy functions that work on every element.

In this chapter, you will learn to:

  • get one element of an array with an index (a[0], a[-1]), and several elements with a slice (a[1:4], a[::2]);

  • change an element, and avoid two surprises: decimals that disappear in an integer array, and a slice that changes the original array;

  • do arithmetic between two arrays of the same length, and between an array and a number;

  • read the error that two arrays of different lengths give;

  • use np.round, np.abs and np.sqrt on a whole array.

import numpy as np

As in the previous chapter, the first cell imports NumPy with the short name np. Run it once, before the other cells.

4.2. One element: indexing#

To get one element, write its position in square brackets after the array’s name. The position is called the index. The coffee beans are at position 0:

prices[0]

np.float64(12.0)

Colab shows np.float64(12.0). This is the number 12.0, stored as a NumPy float64 (the dtype of the array, from the previous chapter). In Thonny, print(prices[0]) shows just 12.0. We can use it like any other number. For example, here is the revenue of the coffee grinder, at position 3:

prices[3] * units[3]

np.float64(60.0)

30.00 × 2 = 60.0 dollars. A negative index counts from the end: -1 is the last element, -2 the one before it, and so on. The last product is the mug:

prices[-1]

np.float64(15.5)

With -1, we do not need to know how long the array is.

Common mistake: writing prices[6] for the sixth product. The positions are 0 to 5, so the sixth product is prices[5] (or prices[-1]). prices[6] gives the error IndexError: index 6 is out of bounds for axis 0 with size 6: there is no position 6 in an array of size 6. (Axis 0 is the one direction of a 1-D array; the chapter Two-dimensional arrays explains axes.)

4.3. Several elements: slicing#

To get several neighboring elements, write a slice: a start and a stop, with a colon between them. Here are the prices at positions 1, 2 and 3:

prices[1:4]

array([ 4.5 ,  7.25, 30.  ])

A slice gives a new, shorter array: the prices of the tea, the chocolate and the grinder. As with np.arange and with list slices, the stop is not included: 1:4 means the positions 1, 2 and 3.

Leave out the start to begin at the first element. Here are the first three prices:

prices[:3]

array([12.  ,  4.5 ,  7.25])

Leave out the stop to go to the end. prices[3:] gives the prices from position 3 to the end, the last three.

A slice can also have a third number, the step, as in start:stop:step. With a step of 2, we get every second element. Here we leave out the start and the stop, so the slice covers the whole array:

prices[::2]

array([12.  ,  7.25,  2.75])

The elements at positions 0, 2 and 4: the coffee beans, the chocolate and the cookies.

4.4. Changing an element#

We can also change an element: put the index on the left of =. Another shop, a gift shop, keeps its prices in whole dollars. The price of its third product drops from 15 to 12 dollars:

gift_prices = np.array([25, 40, 15, 60])
gift_prices[2] = 12
gift_prices

array([25, 40, 12, 60])

The element at position 2 is now 12. The other elements did not change.

Now the gift shop changes its first price from 25 to 24.99:

gift_prices[0] = 24.99
gift_prices

array([24, 40, 12, 60])

The price became 24, not 24.99!

Common mistake: putting a float into an integer array. The dtype of gift_prices is int64, because we made it from whole numbers, and an array holds one type. So NumPy cuts off the decimal part of 24.99: it does not even round it to 25. There is no error and no warning. If an array may need decimals, make it from floats at the start: np.array([25.0, 40.0, 15.0, 60.0]).

4.5. A slice is a view#

Here is a surprise. Let’s take a slice of gift_prices, and change an element of the slice:

first_two = gift_prices[:2]
first_two[0] = 0
gift_prices

array([ 0, 40, 12, 60])

gift_prices changed too: its first price is now 0. A slice of an array is a view: a window onto the same elements, not a copy of them. Changing the view changes the original array. NumPy works this way to save time and memory with large arrays. (A slice of a list is different: it is a copy.)

When you want an independent array, add .copy(). First, let’s put the price back to 25:

gift_prices[0] = 25
first_two = gift_prices[:2].copy()
first_two[0] = 0
gift_prices

array([25, 40, 12, 60])

This time, first_two is a copy, and gift_prices keeps its price of 25.

4.6. Arithmetic between two arrays#

Now back to the six products of the shop. The revenue of a product is its units sold times its price. With two arrays of the same length, one line computes the revenue of every product:

revenue = units * prices
revenue

array([120. , 112.5,  58. ,  60. , 110. ,  93. ])

NumPy multiplies the two arrays element by element: the first element by the first element (10 × 12.00 = 120 for the coffee beans), the second by the second (25 × 4.50 = 112.5 for the tea), and so on. The result has one revenue for each product. units holds integers and prices holds floats, so the result holds floats.

The other operators work the same way: +, -, / and **. For example, here is what each product costs the shop, and the profit on one unit of each:

costs = np.array([7.00, 2.00, 4.00, 18.00, 1.25, 6.50])
prices - costs

array([ 5.  ,  2.5 ,  3.25, 12.  ,  1.5 ,  9.  ])

The shop earns \(12.00 on each grinder it sells, but only \)1.50 on a box of cookies.

To add up the revenue of all the products, we can use .sum() (the next chapter shows this and other summaries):

revenue.sum()

np.float64(553.5)

The shop’s revenue last week was $553.50.

Try it: compute what the units sold last week cost the shop, for each product: units * costs.

4.7. Arithmetic with a number#

An array and a single number work together too: NumPy uses the number with every element. Here are the prices during a sale with 10% off (we pay 90% of each price):

prices * 0.9

array([10.8  ,  4.05 ,  6.525, 27.   ,  2.475, 13.95 ])

The coffee beans now cost 10.80 dollars instead of 12.00, and every other price is 10% lower too. This is what we did in the previous chapter with prices * 1.08, and in the chapter What does a model learn? with 700 * customers["visits_per_month"]. The other operators work with a number in the same way.

Try it: raise every price by 50 cents with prices + 0.50. Then compute the units sold if every product sold 5 more, with units + 5.

4.8. Arrays of different lengths#

What if the two arrays do not have the same length? The manager wrote down this week’s units for only the first three products, and multiplies them by the six prices:

this_week = np.array([12, 20, 9])
this_week * prices

---------------------------------------------------------------------------
ValueError                                Traceback (most recent call last)
Cell In[17], line 2
      1 this_week = np.array([12, 20, 9])
----> 2 this_week * prices

ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together with shapes (3,) (6,)

The last line of the error message is ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together with shapes (3,) (6,). In plain words:

  • the operands are the two arrays on the two sides of *;

  • broadcast is NumPy’s word for matching up the elements of the two operands;

  • the shapes (3,) and (6,) say that one array has 3 elements and the other has 6.

NumPy cannot pair 3 elements with 6, and it does not guess which products the three numbers belong to. The fix is to give it arrays of the same length: for example, the units of all six products, or a slice of the first three prices, this_week * prices[:3].

4.9. NumPy functions for whole arrays#

NumPy has many functions that work on every element of an array. Here are three.

Rounding. The shop also sells online in Europe. With an exchange rate of 0.9137 euros for one dollar, the prices in euros have many decimals:

euros = prices * 0.9137
euros

array([10.9644  ,  4.11165 ,  6.624325, 27.411   ,  2.512675, 14.16235 ])

np.round(a, 2) rounds every element to 2 decimals, as prices should be:

np.round(euros, 2)

array([10.96,  4.11,  6.62, 27.41,  2.51, 14.16])

The coffee beans cost 10.96 euros, the tea 4.11 euros, and so on.

Absolute values. Last week, the shop had a forecast of the units it would sell. The error of the forecast is the real value minus the forecast (as in the chapter What does a model learn?):

forecast = np.array([12, 20, 10, 2, 35, 8])
errors = units - forecast
errors

array([-2,  5, -2,  0,  5, -2])

A negative error means that the forecast was too high: the shop expected 12 bags of coffee beans and sold 10, an error of -2. Often we care only about how big an error is, not about its sign. np.abs gives the absolute value of every element, the value without its sign:

np.abs(errors)

array([2, 5, 2, 0, 5, 2])

The forecast was off by 2, 5, 2, 0, 5 and 2 units. The mean absolute error (MAE) of the chapter A first machine-learning example is the average of such numbers.

Square roots. In the chapter What does a model learn?, we squared the errors. np.sqrt gives the square root of every element, so it undoes a square:

squared = errors ** 2
np.sqrt(squared)

array([2., 5., 2., 0., 5., 2.])

The square roots are the sizes of the errors again: 2, 5, 2, 0, 5 and 2. The result holds floats (2. is 2.0), because a square root is usually a decimal number: the square root of 2 is about 1.414.

4.10. Summary#

  • Indexing: a[0] is the first element and a[-1] the last. Positions start at 0.

  • Slicing: a[start:stop:step] gives several elements as an array: a[1:4], a[:3], a[3:], a[::2]. The stop is not included.

  • Changing an element: a[0] = 25. A float put into an integer array is cut off (24.99 becomes 24), without a warning.

  • A slice is a view: changing it changes the original array. Use a[:2].copy() for an independent array.

  • Arithmetic between two arrays of the same length works element by element: units * prices gives the revenue of each product. Arrays of different lengths give a ValueError (could not be broadcast).

  • Arithmetic with a number works on every element: prices * 0.9, prices + 0.50.

  • Functions on every element: np.round(a, 2), np.abs(a), np.sqrt(a). a.sum() adds up all the elements.

  • “NumPy: the absolute basics for beginners”, from the NumPy documentation, the sections on indexing and slicing and on basic array operations: https://numpy.org/doc/stable/user/absolute_beginners.html.

4.12.5. Question 5 (Medium)#

A deli keeps the kilograms of three cheeses in stock: stock = np.array([3, 5, 2]). After a delivery, it sets stock[1] = 4.5. What does stock show, and why? How should the deli make the array, so that 4.5 is kept?

Answer

stock shows 3, 4 and 2: the array holds integers (int64), so NumPy cuts 4.5 to 4. If the array is made from floats, its dtype is float64, and it keeps 4.5.

stock = np.array([3, 5, 2])
stock[1] = 4.5
print(stock)

stock = np.array([3.0, 5.0, 2.0])
stock[1] = 4.5
print(stock)

4.12.6. Question 6 (Hard)#

What does prices show after these two lines? Explain, then check. How would you change the first line so that prices does not change?

cheap = prices[4:]
cheap[0] = 3.00
Answer

cheap is a view of the last two prices, so cheap[0] is the same element as prices[4]. The price of the cookies in prices becomes 3.0. With cheap = prices[4:].copy(), cheap is an independent array, and prices does not change.

cheap = prices[4:]
cheap[0] = 3.00
print(prices)

4.12.8. Question 8 (Challenge)#

How big was each forecast error compared with the real units? Compute the size of each error (without its sign) as a percentage of the units sold, rounded to 1 decimal. For which product was the forecast worst?

Answer

Divide the absolute errors by the units sold, and multiply by 100. The percentages are 20.0, 20.0, 25.0, 0.0, 12.5 and 33.3. The forecast was worst for the mug (position 5): it was off by 2 of the 6 units sold, 33.3%. (The next chapter shows how to find the position of the largest value with code.)

percent = np.abs(units - forecast) / units * 100
np.round(percent, 1)
